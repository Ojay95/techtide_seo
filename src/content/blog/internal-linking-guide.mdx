Imagine your website as a building. Each page is a room. Internal links are the doors and corridors between them. If a room has no door, nobody can find it. If one room has a hundred doors leading to it, everyone assumes it's important. And if the signs on the doors say "click here", visitors have no idea what's inside.

That's internal linking. It's one of the most powerful and most overlooked parts of SEO, partly because it's free, completely in your control and doesn't need a developer. A few hours of thoughtful linking can lift pages that were stuck on page two or three.

This guide explains what internal links are, why they matter, how to plan a simple structure, how to write good link text and how to find and fix the gaps on your own site. No jargon, and everything is something you can do this week.

## What internal links are

An internal link is a link from one page on your website to another page on the same website. Your menu, footer, breadcrumbs, "related posts" boxes and the links inside your text are all internal links. Links from other websites to yours are called backlinks, which is a different topic. See our guide to [safe link building](/blog/how-to-get-backlinks/) for those.

There are two main kinds:

- **Navigational links:** menus, footers and breadcrumbs that help people move around.
- **Contextual links:** links placed inside the body of a page, where they fit naturally. These are often the most valuable, because the surrounding words explain what the linked page is about.

## Why internal links matter for SEO

**They help Google find your pages.** Search engines discover pages by following links. A page with no internal links, called an orphan, may never be found or may be crawled rarely. Google notes that it can only find pages through links it can follow, which is why its guide on [making links crawlable](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/links-crawlable) is worth a read.

**They show which pages matter most.** Pages that receive many links from other relevant pages tend to be treated as more important within your site.

**They give context.** The words you use to link tell search engines and readers what the linked page covers.

**They keep visitors moving.** A good link answers the next question a reader has. They stay longer, see more of your content and are more likely to contact you.

**They spread the authority you've earned.** When another site links to one of your pages, internal links help pass some of that value on to related pages.

## A simple structure: pillar and supporting pages

You don't need a complex plan. A hub-and-spoke structure works for almost any site.

1. **Choose a main topic**, such as "local SEO".
2. **Create or pick a pillar page** that covers the topic broadly. This might be a service page or a long guide.
3. **Create supporting pages** that go deeper on specific parts: reviews, citations, your Google profile and so on.
4. **Link supporting pages to the pillar** and the pillar to each supporting page.
5. **Link related supporting pages to each other** where it genuinely helps the reader.

For a service business, a typical structure looks like this:

- **Service page** (where you sell): "Local SEO services"
- **Guides** (where you teach): "Complete local SEO guide", "Google Business Profile optimisation", "How to get more reviews"
- **Proof**: a case study or testimonials page
- **FAQ**: quick answers that link out to the detailed pages

Each guide links to the service page with a natural call to action. The service page links to two or three guides to answer common questions. That's the same pattern we use on this site. See, for example, our [local SEO guide](/guides/local-seo-guide/) and [local SEO service](/services/local-seo/).

## How to write good link text (anchor text)

The clickable words in a link are called anchor text. They matter, because they tell people and search engines what to expect.

**Good anchor text is:**
- descriptive: "our guide to Core Web Vitals" rather than "this page",
- natural: it reads smoothly in the sentence,
- varied: you don't need to use the exact same phrase every time,
- short enough to scan, usually two to six words.

**Avoid:**
- "click here", "read more" and "this link", which say nothing,
- stuffing the same exact keyword into every link, which looks forced,
- very long sentences as links,
- linking whole paragraphs.

## How many internal links should a page have?

There's no magic number. A useful rule is to add links wherever a reader would reasonably want to learn more, and no more. A 1,500-word article might have five to fifteen internal links. A short page might have two or three. If a paragraph looks like a wall of blue, you've gone too far.

Place the most important links near the top of the page, where more people will see them, and avoid hiding all your links in the footer.

## How to find internal linking opportunities

You don't need paid tools, though they help.

**Method 1: Look at your most important pages first.** List your top five to ten pages, usually the services and products that earn money. Check how many other pages link to each. Many will have only the menu link.

**Method 2: Use Search Console.** The Links report in [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console) shows your top internally linked pages. It's a quick way to see which pages receive the most links and which receive few.

**Method 3: Search your own site.** In Google, type `site:yourwebsite.com "local SEO"` (replace with your site and topic). Every result is a page that mentions the topic, and a candidate to link to your main page on it.

**Method 4: Use a crawler.** A tool like [Screaming Frog](https://www.screamingfrog.co.uk/seo-spider/) crawls your site and shows how many internal links each page has, and which pages have none.

## A 30-minute routine you can repeat

1. **Pick one important page** that you want to rank, such as a service page.
2. **Find five to ten related pages** that mention its topic, using the site search trick above.
3. **Open each one** and add a natural link to the important page, using descriptive anchor text.
4. **Add links the other way** from the important page to two or three supporting guides.
5. **Check the links work** and open the right pages.
6. **Note the date**, then check the page's performance in Search Console in four to six weeks.

Do this weekly for your top pages, and monthly across the site.

## Fix orphan pages first

Orphan pages have no internal links pointing to them. They're usually old posts, landing pages created and forgotten, or pages missing from the menu. Find them with a crawler or by comparing your sitemap against your linked pages. Then decide for each one:

- **Keep it:** add links from at least two relevant pages.
- **Merge it:** combine it with a stronger page and redirect the old address.
- **Remove it:** if it's out of date or thin, delete it and redirect it.

## Update old posts when you publish new ones

It's easy to link from new content to old content. The harder, more valuable habit is the reverse: when you publish a new page, go back and link to it from older, relevant ones. Search your site for related terms and add links. That way the new page gets discovered and supported straight away.

## Breadcrumbs, menus and footers

Navigation links help too, as long as you keep them focused.

- **Menu:** link only your most important pages. Too many items makes everything look equally unimportant.
- **Breadcrumbs:** these show the path to the current page, such as Home > Guides > Local SEO. They help readers and give search engines clear structure. Our site uses them.
- **Footer:** useful for legal pages, contact details and key services, but don't stuff it with dozens of links.
- **Related posts:** a short box of two or three genuinely related pages works better than a long auto-generated list.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Linking to pages that redirect or return errors.** Link straight to the final, working address.
- **Using nofollow on your own pages.** There's rarely a reason to do this internally.
- **Stuffing exact-match anchors.** It reads unnaturally.
- **Linking everything to everything.** Links should make sense for the reader.
- **Ignoring mobile.** Make sure links are easy to tap and not too close together.
- **Linking only from the footer.** Contextual links inside content carry more meaning.
- **Never updating links** after you change or delete a page.
- **Burying important pages** more than three clicks from the home page.

## How to tell whether it's working

Internal linking effects are gradual. Over the next one to three months, look for:

- more pages indexed, if you were fixing orphans,
- more impressions and clicks to the pages you strengthened,
- improved positions for target searches in Search Console,
- more visitors moving from guides to service pages.

Keep a simple note of what you changed and when, so you can match it to results.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many internal links are too many?
If links crowd the text or stop making sense, there are too many. Focus on relevance and usefulness rather than a number.

### Do internal links pass "link juice"?
They help pass authority and context between your pages, and they help crawlers discover content. Treat that as a benefit of linking well, not a trick.

### Should internal links open in a new tab?
Usually no. Keeping visitors in the same tab is the normal, accessible choice for internal links.

### Can I use the same anchor text more than once?
Yes, when it's natural. Just avoid repeating the exact same keyword-heavy phrase everywhere.

### How is this different from backlinks?
Internal links connect pages on your own site. Backlinks come from other sites. Both matter, and internal links are the ones you fully control.
