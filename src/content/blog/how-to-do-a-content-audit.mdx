Most websites collect content the way a garage collects boxes. A blog post here, a page there, an old offer nobody remembers. For a while it doesn't matter. Then one day you realise half your site is out of date, a few pages are quietly competing with each other and nobody can say which ones actually bring in customers.

A content audit fixes that. You go through every page, look at the facts and decide what each one should become. It sounds like a big job, and for a large site it is. But for most small business websites, it takes a weekend. And it's one of the most reliable ways to lift your SEO without writing a single new article.

This guide shows you how to run a content audit step by step, how to make the keep, update, merge or remove decisions and what to do afterwards. You'll need a spreadsheet, a free Google account and a bit of patience.

## What a content audit is

A content audit is a review of all the pages on your site. For each page you ask a few plain questions:

- Is it still accurate?
- Does anyone read it, or find it through search?
- Does it help the business?
- Does it overlap with another page?

Then you decide what to do about it. The audit isn't about deleting things. It's about making sure every page has a clear job and does it well.

## Why audits improve SEO

Google's guidance on [helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) asks a simple question: is your content created for people and genuinely useful? An audit helps you answer it honestly.

Here's what usually happens when you run one:

- **Old pages get refreshed**, and refreshed pages often climb back up the results.
- **Overlapping pages get merged**, which stops them competing with each other. We explain that problem in our guide to [internal linking](/blog/internal-linking-guide/).
- **Thin, pointless pages get removed or improved**, so the site as a whole looks more useful.
- **Gaps appear**, which gives you a clear list of new pages worth writing.

It also saves money. Updating a page that already has links and traffic is usually cheaper and faster than starting from nothing.

## Before you start: decide what you want

Pick one or two goals, so your decisions have a direction:

- more enquiries or sales from existing pages,
- better rankings for your main services,
- a smaller, cleaner site that's easier to maintain,
- a plan for next quarter's content.

Write the goal at the top of your spreadsheet. It will help you decide when a page is borderline.

## Step 1: List every page

You need a complete list of your pages. Use whichever method suits you.

- **Your sitemap.** Open `yourwebsite.com/sitemap.xml` and copy the addresses. Our site uses a sitemap index, which points to a second file with the pages.
- **Your CMS.** Most platforms let you export pages and posts.
- **A crawler.** [Screaming Frog](https://www.screamingfrog.co.uk/seo-spider/) crawls your site and exports every address, title and word count. The free version handles up to 500 pages.
- **Google Search Console.** The Pages report shows which pages Google has indexed.

Paste everything into a spreadsheet, one page per row. Keep the first column for the address.

## Step 2: Add the data that matters

A spreadsheet with only addresses isn't much use. Add columns for the facts you need to decide. You don't need all of these, but the first five are the most valuable:

1. **Title** (from your crawl).
2. **Word count**, to spot very thin pages.
3. **Clicks and impressions** over the last 90 days, from [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console).
4. **Sessions or views** from Google Analytics, if you use it.
5. **Last updated date.**
6. **Number of backlinks**, from Search Console's Links report or a link tool.
7. **Main topic or keyword**, so you can spot overlaps.
8. **Page type**: service page, guide, news, product and so on.

To get clicks per page in Search Console, open Performance, choose the Pages tab and set the date range to the last three months. Export the table and match it to your list. Our [Search Console guide](/blog/how-to-use-google-search-console/) shows how.

## Step 3: Find the pages that are slipping

Before you decide anything, check which pages have lost traffic. In Search Console, compare the last three months with the previous three. Pages with a big drop are often the best candidates for an update, because they used to perform and may only need freshening.

![Screenshot: Search Console pages report comparing two periods and showing clicks gained and lost](/images/screenshots/search-console-pages-declining.png)

Add a column called "Change in clicks" to your sheet. It tells you where the best quick wins are.

## Step 4: Decide what to do with each page

Now go through the sheet row by row and choose one of four actions. Here's a simple way to decide.

| Decision | When to use it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Keep** | The page performs well, is accurate and has a clear purpose | A service page with steady clicks and enquiries |
| **Update** | The topic still matters, but the page is old, thin or slipping | A guide from 2019 with outdated advice |
| **Merge** | Two or more pages cover the same topic | Three short posts called "SEO tips part 1, 2, 3" |
| **Remove** | The page has no traffic, no links and no purpose | A news post about an office party from 2018 |

A few rules of thumb make the decisions easier.

**Keep pages that already work.** If a page brings visitors or leads, leave it alone, or make only small improvements. Don't fix what isn't broken.

**Update pages with potential.** Look for pages that get impressions but few clicks, or sit between positions eight and thirty in Search Console. They're close to doing well.

**Merge pages that compete.** When two pages target the same search, Google may not know which to show. Combine the best parts into one stronger page and redirect the other. This problem is called keyword cannibalisation.

**Remove carefully.** A page with zero traffic isn't automatically worthless. Check for backlinks first. If it has links, redirect it to the closest relevant page instead of deleting it.

## Step 5: Do the work

Now act, starting with the pages that matter most to your business.

### Updating a page

1. Read it as a stranger would. Is it accurate and clear?
2. Fix anything out of date: prices, names, steps, screenshots, laws.
3. Answer the main question near the top. See [how to write SEO content](/blog/how-to-write-seo-content/).
4. Improve the title and description. Our guide on [title tags and meta descriptions](/blog/title-tags-and-meta-descriptions-that-get-clicks/) has templates.
5. Add internal links to and from related pages.
6. Add a real example, photo or number that makes it better than before.
7. Change the "last updated" date, but only if you really changed the content.

### Merging pages

1. Choose the stronger page to keep. It's usually the one with more links and traffic.
2. Copy useful sections from the weaker pages into it.
3. Redirect each old address to the kept page with a permanent (301) redirect. Google explains how in its guide to [redirects](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/301-redirects).
4. Update internal links so they point straight to the kept page.

### Removing a page

- If it has links or some traffic, redirect it to the nearest relevant page.
- If it has none and no replacement fits, let it return a "gone" or "not found" response. Google's guide to [HTTP status codes](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/http-network-errors) explains what each one means.
- Remove it from your menus, sitemap and internal links.

Never bulk-delete pages without checking each one. It's easy to remove something that quietly brought in customers.

## Step 6: Plan new content from the gaps

An audit doesn't only clean things up. It shows what's missing. Look at your sheet and ask:

- Which services have no page of their own?
- What questions do customers ask that no page answers?
- Which topics have only one thin post, but plenty of search interest?

Add these to a content plan and use [keyword research](/blog/how-to-do-keyword-research/) to choose the best ones first.

## Step 7: Measure the results

Give changes time. For most sites, four to eight weeks is enough to see movement. Then check:

- clicks and impressions for the updated pages,
- positions for target searches,
- enquiries, calls or sales from those pages,
- the number of indexed pages in Search Console.

Keep notes about what you changed and the date, so you can learn what worked.

## A realistic example

Imagine a plumbing company with 60 pages. After a content audit they find:

- 14 pages performing well, which they keep,
- 18 old posts, which they update,
- 9 near-duplicate posts, merged into 3,
- 11 pages with no traffic or links, which they remove,
- 8 services with no page of their own, added to the content plan.

The site shrinks to about 50 pages, but every page has a clear job. Within two months, several of the updated pages move onto page one for local searches. The key isn't doing more. It's making what you have better.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Judging only by traffic.** A page with few visits might drive valuable enquiries or support other pages.
- **Deleting pages with backlinks.** You lose the authority those links carry.
- **Forgetting redirects.** Old addresses end up as dead ends.
- **Updating only the date.** Google and readers can tell when nothing changed.
- **Trying to fix everything at once.** Start with the top 20 percent of pages.
- **Never repeating it.** Content gets old. Audit once or twice a year.

## How often should you audit?

Small sites benefit from a full audit once a year and a quick check every quarter. Larger or fast-moving sites, such as shops and news sites, may do it more often. Set a calendar reminder so it doesn't slip.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does a content audit take?
For a site with about 50 to 100 pages, plan on one to two days. Bigger sites take longer, so start with your most important sections.

### Do I need paid tools?
No. A spreadsheet, Google Search Console and a free crawler cover most small sites.

### Should I delete old blog posts?
Not automatically. Update the ones with potential, merge duplicates and remove only the pages with no traffic, no links and no purpose.

### Will a content audit improve my rankings?
It often helps, because it improves quality and clears out overlap. Results depend on your starting point and market, and nobody can promise a specific ranking.

### How many pages should I update first?
Pick the ten pages closest to your business goals, or the ten that lost the most clicks. Do those first.
