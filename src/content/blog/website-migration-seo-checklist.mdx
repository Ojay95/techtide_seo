A new website is exciting. A fresh design, better photos, a cleaner menu, maybe a new platform. For a few weeks everyone on the team is happy, until the phone stops ringing.

It's one of the most common and most expensive stories in SEO. A business launches a beautiful new site, and within weeks its traffic falls by a third, or half, or worse. Nothing was "wrong" with the design. The problem was what happened behind the scenes: addresses changed, redirects were missing, a hidden setting told Google to stay away.

The good news is that migration problems are almost entirely preventable. This guide is a practical checklist for moving a website without losing the visibility you've built. It covers planning, preparation, launch day and the months after, in plain language.

## What counts as a migration

A migration is any big change that affects how search engines find and understand your site. Common types:

- **Redesign** with new page layouts and URLs.
- **New platform or CMS**, such as moving from one website builder to another.
- **Domain change**, from one web address to another.
- **Switching to HTTPS.**
- **Merging sites** or moving a blog into the main site.
- **Changing URL structure**, even on the same domain.

The more addresses change, the bigger the risk. A design refresh that keeps every address the same is much safer than a rebuild that renames everything.

Google has dedicated guides for the two main cases: [site moves with URL changes](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes) and [site moves without URL changes](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-no-url-changes). They're worth reading before you start.

## The golden rules

1. **Change as little as you can.** Every changed address is a risk.
2. **Keep what works.** Pages that rank, bring leads or earn links should keep their content, titles and headings, wherever possible.
3. **Redirect every old address** that matters to its closest new page.
4. **Test everything before launch.**
5. **Monitor closely afterwards.**

## Phase 1: Plan (4 to 8 weeks before)

### 1. Set goals and a timeline

Decide why you're migrating and what success looks like. Avoid launching during your busiest season, and don't schedule it right before a holiday when nobody is around to fix problems.

### 2. Record your baseline

Save how things look today, so you can compare later:

- Organic clicks, impressions and positions from [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console).
- Traffic and leads from Google Analytics.
- Your top 50 pages by traffic and by links.
- Rankings for your most important searches.
- Core Web Vitals and speed scores.
- A full crawl of the current site.

### 3. List every current page

Export all addresses from your sitemap, a crawler such as [Screaming Frog](https://www.screamingfrog.co.uk/seo-spider/) and Search Console. Add columns for traffic, backlinks and importance. This is your starting inventory.

### 4. Decide what moves, merges or goes

For each page, decide whether to keep it, improve it, merge it with another or retire it. Our guide to [content audits](/blog/how-to-do-a-content-audit/) helps you make those calls.

## Phase 2: Build in private

### 5. Use a staging site, and keep it hidden

Build the new site on a separate test address. Protect it with a password, or block it with noindex, so Google doesn't index it. Many migrations go wrong because the test site was indexed, or because the hidden setting was left on at launch.

### 6. Keep your SEO foundations

Make sure the new site includes:

- **The same or better content** on important pages.
- **Unique titles and meta descriptions.** See [title tags and meta descriptions](/blog/title-tags-and-meta-descriptions-that-get-clicks/).
- **Clear headings** and the same main topics.
- **Internal links** between related pages. See [internal linking](/blog/internal-linking-guide/).
- **Image alt text** and compressed images.
- **Structured data**, such as Organization and Breadcrumb. See [schema markup](/blog/schema-markup-guide/).
- **Mobile-friendly design.** See [mobile SEO](/blog/mobile-seo-guide/).
- **Fast loading.** See [Core Web Vitals](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained/).

### 7. Keep the URL structure where possible

If your addresses were clean and sensible, keep them. If you must change them, keep them short, readable and consistent.

### 8. Map every redirect

This is the most important step. For every old address that has traffic, links or value, choose the matching new address. Record the pairs in a spreadsheet.

![Screenshot: a redirect map spreadsheet listing old addresses, new addresses and the test status of each](/images/screenshots/redirect-map-spreadsheet.png)

*Figure 2: A redirect map pairs every old address with its closest new page and records a test result.*

Redirect rules:

- Use **permanent (301) redirects**. Google explains them in its [redirect guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/301-redirects).
- Send each old page to its **closest equivalent**, not everything to the home page.
- Avoid **redirect chains** (A to B to C). Point straight to the final page.
- Include **parameter and old-style addresses** if they received links or traffic.
- If a page has no equivalent, redirect to the nearest relevant category or let it return "not found".

### 9. Set up tracking

Check that Analytics, tag manager, conversion events and call or chat tracking work on the new site before launch. Our guide to [tracking SEO results](/blog/track-seo-results-ga4-search-console/) explains how.

## Phase 3: Pre-launch checks

Before you flip the switch, test on the staging site:

- Crawl it, and fix broken links, missing titles and errors.
- Test every redirect in your map.
- Check that robots.txt will allow search engines at launch, and that the staging protection can be removed.
- Check canonical tags point to the new, final addresses.
- Check forms, booking, checkout and phone links on desktop and mobile.
- Check the XML sitemap lists only the new, working pages.
- Check speed and mobile scores.
- Prepare an updated list of your profiles and listings to change.

## Phase 4: Launch day

Choose a quiet time, and have your team ready.

1. **Remove the staging protection and noindex settings.** This is the step people forget.
2. **Switch the site live and activate redirects.**
3. **Test the redirects again on the live site**, starting with your top pages.
4. **Check robots.txt** and confirm important pages aren't blocked.
5. **Submit the new sitemap** in Search Console.
6. **If you changed domain,** use the Change of address tool in Search Console after redirects are working.

![Screenshot: the Change of address tool with steps for selecting the new site and verifying redirects](/images/screenshots/search-console-change-of-address.png)

*Figure 3: For a domain move, tell Google about it once the redirects are in place.*

7. **Update links you control:** Google Business Profile, social profiles, email signatures, directories and advertising.
8. **Check analytics** is recording visits and key events.
9. **Request indexing** for key pages with URL inspection.

## Phase 5: Monitor for three months

The weeks after launch decide whether the migration succeeded.

**Daily for the first week:**
- Search Console: coverage and indexing errors.
- Crawl errors and broken links.
- Traffic and leads against your baseline.

**Weekly for three months:**
- Clicks, impressions and positions for key pages.
- Pages that dropped, and why.
- Indexed page counts.
- Core Web Vitals.
- New errors, redirect problems and broken links.
- Calls, forms and bookings.

**Fix problems quickly.** A missing redirect or a blocked page found in week one is easy to repair. Found in month three, it may have cost you thousands of visits.

Keep redirects in place for at least a year, and ideally longer. Our [Search Console guide](/blog/how-to-use-google-search-console/) explains the reports to watch.

## What's a normal dip?

Some movement is common after a migration, as Google recrawls and reprocesses the site. A small, short dip that recovers within weeks can be normal. A large, lasting fall means something is wrong, and you should check redirects, indexing, content changes and technical settings first. Nobody can promise zero change, but careful work keeps it small.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving noindex or password protection on after launch.**
- **Not redirecting old addresses**, or redirecting everything to the home page.
- **Changing every URL for no reason.**
- **Removing content that ranked.**
- **Changing titles and headings across the board** during the same project.
- **Forgetting mobile and speed testing.**
- **Launching without tracking.**
- **Not telling the team** about the change.
- **Launching in peak season or on a Friday afternoon.**
- **Switching off the old site too soon.**

## A quick checklist

**Before:** baseline recorded, page list built, redirect map complete, staging protected, tracking ready, content preserved.

**Launch:** protection removed, redirects live and tested, robots.txt checked, sitemap submitted, change of address done (if needed).

**After:** daily checks first week, weekly checks for three months, fix issues fast, keep redirects for a year.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to recover after a migration?
It varies. With careful redirects and content, many sites see only a short dip. If problems exist, recovery takes as long as it takes to find and fix them.

### Do I need to redirect every old page?
Redirect every page that has traffic, links or value. Pages with none can be dropped, though redirecting is usually harmless.

### Should I change my domain?
Only if you have a strong reason, such as a rebrand. Domain changes carry more risk than a redesign.

### Can I migrate during a busy season?
Avoid it. If something goes wrong, you'll feel it most when demand is highest.

### Do I need an SEO specialist?
For small, simple moves, you may manage with this checklist. For larger or high-value sites, an expert review of your plan before launch is worth the cost.
