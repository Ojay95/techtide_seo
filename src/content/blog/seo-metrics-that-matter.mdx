Open any SEO report and you'll find a wall of numbers. Impressions, average position, domain scores, keyword counts, bounce rates, backlinks. Some are going up, some are going down, and most of them come with a coloured arrow that makes you feel good or bad.

Here's the uncomfortable truth. Most of those numbers don't pay your bills. A business can double its "keywords ranked" and not get a single extra phone call. Another can get a tiny rise in traffic and win three new customers. The difference is knowing which numbers are connected to money and which are just decoration.

This guide shows you the SEO metrics that matter, the ones that give useful context and the ones you can safely ignore. You'll also get a simple way to build a one-page report that you'll actually read. No jargon, and nothing you need a data degree for.

## The one rule: connect every number to a result

Before we list any metrics, here's the test we use. For every number in a report, ask: **"If this goes up, what happens to the business?"**

If the answer is "more customers" or "more sales", it's a metric that matters. If the answer is "it looks nice" or "nothing, really", it's a vanity metric.

## Group 1: Metrics that matter most

These are the numbers closest to your goals. If you track only a few things, track these.

### 1. Leads from organic search

A lead is anyone who contacts you or takes a step towards buying: a form, a phone call, a WhatsApp message, a booking request. Count how many come from people who found you through search.

**Where to find it:** Google Analytics, using key events, plus call and chat tracking. We explain how in our guide to [tracking SEO results](/blog/track-seo-results-ga4-search-console/).

### 2. Calls, messages and direction requests from your Google profile

For local businesses, your Google Business Profile often brings more action than your website. It shows calls, direction requests, website clicks and messages.

**Where to find it:** the performance section of your profile. See our [profile guide](/guides/google-business-profile-optimization/).

### 3. Conversion rate from organic visitors

This is the share of organic visitors who become leads. If 1,000 people visit and 25 enquire, that's a 2.5 percent conversion rate. It tells you whether your pages persuade people, not just attract them.

### 4. Revenue or value from organic customers

If you can connect leads to sales, even roughly, you'll know what SEO is worth. A simple version: ask new customers how they found you, and record it.

### 5. Clicks and click-through rate for your key pages

Clicks from Google Search Console show real visits from search. Click-through rate, clicks divided by impressions, shows whether your titles and descriptions work. Our guide to [title tags and meta descriptions](/blog/title-tags-and-meta-descriptions-that-get-clicks/) shows how to improve it.

## Group 2: Useful context

These numbers aren't goals, but they help you understand why results are moving.

**Impressions.** How often your pages appeared in results. Rising impressions suggest growing visibility. On their own, they don't earn money.

**Average position.** Where you typically appear. It's helpful for individual searches, but misleading as an overall number, because it blends many different searches. Use it per page or per query.

**Branded searches.** People searching your business name. Growth here means more people know you. It's a healthy sign that your brand is building.

**Indexed pages.** How many pages Google has stored. A sudden drop is a warning. Our guide to [getting indexed](/blog/how-to-get-your-website-indexed-faster/) explains what to check.

**Core Web Vitals and page speed.** They show the quality of the visitor experience. See [Core Web Vitals explained](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained/).

**New referring domains.** The number of different sites linking to you. Quality matters more than count. See [safe link building](/blog/how-to-get-backlinks/).

**Reviews and rating.** The number, average and recency of your Google reviews. They influence both rankings and decisions.

## Group 3: Vanity metrics you can mostly ignore

These aren't useless, but they're often over-reported and they rarely tell you whether the business is doing better.

**Total keywords "ranking".** A tool may say you rank for 1,200 keywords. Most are obscure, many sit on page eight and few bring a visitor. A bigger number doesn't mean more customers.

**Raw traffic with no context.** A spike in visits from irrelevant searches doesn't help. Ten visitors who want to buy beat 1,000 who don't.

**Domain scores.** Metrics such as "domain rating" or "domain authority" come from third-party tools. Google doesn't use them. They can be handy for comparing sites, but they aren't a goal.

**Average position as the headline.** It's the most misleading number in many reports. A page can gain impressions on new, low-ranking searches, and "average position" drops even though things improved.

**Bounce rate.** In current versions of Google Analytics, engagement metrics replaced the old idea of bounce rate. A visitor who reads one page and phones you has had a perfect visit.

**Total page views.** Without goals, they say little.

**Social likes and shares.** Good for awareness, but they don't directly affect search rankings.

## Where to get the numbers

You don't need expensive software. These free tools cover almost everything:

- **[Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console):** clicks, impressions, positions, indexing and links. Our [Search Console guide](/blog/how-to-use-google-search-console/) explains the reports.
- **[Google Analytics](https://analytics.google.com):** visits, behaviour and key events such as form submissions and calls.
- **Google Business Profile:** calls, directions, website clicks and reviews.
- **[PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/):** speed and mobile experience.
- **Your own records:** phone logs, WhatsApp chats, booking systems and "how did you hear about us?" answers.

## How to build a one-page report you'll actually read

Many reports are too long to be useful. Here's a simple structure.

**1. Headline results (3 to 5 numbers).** Leads, calls, conversion rate, and value if you have it. Compare with the previous month and the same month last year.

**2. Visibility (3 to 5 numbers).** Clicks, impressions, branded searches and your top pages.

**3. Health (2 to 3 numbers).** Indexed pages, Core Web Vitals and any errors fixed.

**4. What we did.** The work completed this month, in plain words.

**5. What it means.** Two or three sentences: what's working, what isn't, what you'll do next.

**6. Next month.** The plan and anything you need from the client.

## How to read changes without panicking

Numbers move. Here's how to avoid overreacting.

- **Look at trends, not days.** Compare four weeks with the previous four weeks, or with the same month last year.
- **Account for seasons.** A hotel's December isn't comparable with its March.
- **Change one thing at a time**, when you can, so you know what caused a result.
- **Check for tracking breaks.** A sudden zero is often a broken tag, not a disaster.
- **Look at causes.** If clicks fell, find the pages and queries that dropped.
- **Wait for enough data.** Small numbers swing wildly.

## Sample targets for a small business

There's no universal "good" number, so set goals relative to your starting point. For example, a local service business might aim for:

- 20 percent more calls from Google within three months,
- two new reviews a week,
- three key pages moving from page two to page one,
- no pages with "poor" Core Web Vitals.

Targets like these are tied to action. "Get to position one" isn't, because you don't control it.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Reporting only rankings.** They're a means, not an end.
- **Comparing against the wrong period.** Check seasonal effects.
- **Counting every visit as equal.** Quality of traffic matters.
- **Ignoring branded searches.** They show real growth in awareness.
- **Not tracking calls and WhatsApp clicks.** Many leads never touch a form.
- **Changing the way you measure every month.** Keep definitions stable.
- **Paying for reports full of jargon.** If you can't explain it, it isn't useful.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most important SEO metric?
Leads and sales from organic search. Everything else is a step towards those.

### Should I track keyword rankings at all?
Yes, for your most important searches, as an early signal. Just don't treat rankings as the goal. A list of 10 to 30 key searches is enough.

### Are domain authority scores important?
They're third-party estimates, not Google metrics. Use them to compare sites roughly, not as a target.

### How often should I check my numbers?
Glance at Search Console weekly and review a fuller report monthly. Daily checking mostly creates worry.

### What if I can't track leads accurately?
Start simple. Track form submissions, phone calls and chat clicks, and ask new customers how they found you. An imperfect measure is better than none.
