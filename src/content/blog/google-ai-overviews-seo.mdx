You search for something on Google and, before the usual list of links, a box appears with a neat written summary. It answers your question in a few sentences, with a few links tucked beside it. That's an AI Overview.

For people searching, it's convenient. For businesses and website owners, it raises worried questions. Will people still click? How does Google pick which pages to link? Can I get my site in there? And what happens to my traffic if I'm not?

This guide answers those questions in plain English. You'll learn how AI Overviews work, what Google says about being included, practical ways to improve your chances and how to keep growing if some clicks go missing. No hype, and no promises that anyone can honestly make.

## What an AI Overview is

An AI Overview is a short, AI-generated summary that Google shows on some search results pages. It pulls together information from several web pages and links to the sources it used. Google also has "AI Mode", a separate, more conversational way to search where you can ask longer questions and follow up.

Both use generative AI on top of Google's normal search system. They're not a separate internet. The pages they draw from are pages Google has already found, crawled and indexed. That detail matters, because it tells you where to start.

## How an AI Overview is put together

Google hasn't published every detail, but it has explained the broad idea. When someone asks a question, the system can break it into related, smaller questions, search for pages that answer each one, and then write a summary using what it found. Google calls this approach "query fan-out" when describing AI Mode.

That has a practical meaning. A person asking "how do I get more direct bookings for my small hotel" might trigger searches about hotel websites, booking engines, Google Business Profiles and reviews. If you have a clear page on one of those smaller topics, you have a chance of being used, even if you'd never rank first for the big question.

## What Google says about getting included

Google's guidance on [AI features and your website](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) is refreshingly plain. In short:

- There's **no special markup or file** you need to add to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode.
- Your page must be **indexed** and **eligible to appear in Search with a snippet**.
- The same **SEO best practices** still apply: helpful content, a good page experience and clear structure.
- Structured data should **match the visible content** on the page.

That means two things. First, you don't need a new strategy from scratch. Second, anyone selling a secret AI Overview "hack" deserves a lot of scepticism.

## Step 1: Make sure your pages are eligible

Do the checks that every site should do anyway.

1. **Indexing.** Use [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console) to confirm your key pages are indexed. Our guide to [five quick checks](/blog/five-quick-checks-can-google-find-your-website/) shows how.
2. **Snippets allowed.** Pages with a `nosnippet` tag or a very restrictive `max-snippet` setting may be excluded from summaries. Only use those settings if you really mean to.
3. **Crawlable content.** Important text should be in the HTML, not hidden behind scripts or login walls.
4. **Healthy page experience.** Fast, stable, mobile-friendly pages are better for people and for search. See [Core Web Vitals explained](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained/).

## Step 2: Answer the question first

AI Overviews work best with content that gets to the point. Use this simple pattern on informational pages:

- A heading that matches the question, for example "How long does SEO take?"
- A direct answer in one or two sentences.
- The detail, examples and exceptions that follow.

You're not writing for a robot. You're doing what a good teacher does: say the answer, then explain it. People skim, and so do systems.

## Step 3: Cover the follow-up questions

Because AI features explore related questions, build pages that answer the whole cluster, not just one query. For a topic such as local SEO, that might include:

- what it is and how it differs from normal SEO,
- how Google ranks local results,
- how to set up a Business Profile,
- how to get reviews,
- how much it costs and how long it takes.

You can see real follow-up questions in the "People also ask" boxes and in the question-style searches listed in Search Console.

## Step 4: Add specific, original value

A summary can't easily replace a page that offers something unique. Give people a reason to click:

- real prices or ranges,
- your own photos, data and examples,
- a calculator, template or checklist,
- clear comparisons you've researched,
- advice from real experience.

Google's guidance on [people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) talks about showing first-hand experience, expertise and trustworthiness. Add author names and short bios, say how you know what you know and keep the information accurate and up to date.

## Step 5: Use clear structure

Make your pages easy to scan:

- short paragraphs,
- descriptive headings,
- bullet lists for steps and options,
- tables for comparisons,
- a short FAQ section for common questions.

Structure helps readers first. It also makes it easier for systems to pick out the right passage.

## Step 6: Add structured data that matches the page

Markup such as Article, Organization, LocalBusiness, Product and Breadcrumb helps search engines understand your content. It isn't a requirement for AI Overviews, and it won't force a mention, but it's good hygiene. Always make sure the markup reflects what's visible on the page. Our [schema markup guide](/blog/schema-markup-guide/) explains how.

## Step 7: Build trust beyond your own site

Search systems look for signals that a business is real and respected. Reviews, mentions on other sites, links from trusted sources and a consistent business profile all help. ## What about clicks?

This is the worry most site owners have. If Google answers the question on the results page, fewer people may click through. In some cases, that's true. For simple questions with short, factual answers, a summary may be all someone needs.

But the picture isn't the same everywhere:

- Some searches still need a click, such as comparing providers, buying, booking or using a tool.
- Local searches lean on maps, reviews and phone calls, not long summaries.
- Cited links can still attract visitors who want more detail.
- People who do click are often further along in their decision.

Google has said that AI feature traffic is counted in Search Console's Performance report along with other web search results, so you won't see it separated into its own line. Watch your overall clicks, impressions and click-through rate over time, and compare similar pages to spot patterns.

## If your traffic drops: what to do

Don't panic, and don't make big changes after one week of data.

1. **Check whether the drop is real.** Compare the same period to the previous one in Search Console, and look at specific pages and queries.
2. **Find the pages that lost clicks.** Are they simple definition or fact pages? Those are the most likely to be affected.
3. **Improve what can't be summarised easily.** Add tools, local information, original data, checklists and real experience.
4. **Focus on searches that lead to action.** Service pages, comparison pages, pricing and local searches tend to hold up better.
5. **Strengthen your brand.** People who know your name search for it directly, and that traffic doesn't depend on summaries.
6. **Diversify.** Email lists, social profiles, partnerships and a good Google Business Profile all reduce your reliance on any one source.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing a "hack".** There isn't one, according to Google's own guidance.
- **Writing thin definition pages.** They're the easiest content for a summary to replace.
- **Hiding content behind scripts.** If systems can't read it, they can't use it.
- **Ignoring local and conversion pages.** They're often where the business value sits.
- **Making unsupported claims.** Wrong or exaggerated facts hurt trust, and they can hurt rankings too.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I opt out of AI Overviews?
Google offers controls such as `nosnippet`, but using them can reduce how your pages appear in normal results too. Read the [AI features documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) before changing anything.

### Do AI Overviews show on every search?
No. They appear for some queries, not others, and that changes over time. Check the searches that matter to your business.

### Do I need to rank first to be cited?
Not necessarily. Pages cited can come from different positions, and the related sub-searches the system runs mean smaller, specific pages can be used.

### Is AI Mode different from AI Overviews?
They're related. AI Mode is a more conversational search experience that can handle longer questions and follow-ups, while an AI Overview is a summary shown within normal results.

### How can I tell if I've been cited?
Search your key questions yourself, in a private window, and look at the sources listed. There's no dedicated report for it, so a manual check every month is the practical method.
