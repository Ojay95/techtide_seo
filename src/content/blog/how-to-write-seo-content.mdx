Most "SEO content" is bad. You've read it. A page that repeats the same keyword in every sentence, wanders around the point for six paragraphs and ends with "in conclusion, SEO is important". It ranks for nothing and helps nobody.

The sad part is that the writers usually mean well. They were told to "optimise" and they tried, but they forgot who the page is for. Good SEO content is just good writing with a few smart habits behind it. It answers a real question clearly, in plain words, with something useful that the reader couldn't get elsewhere.

This guide shows you a practical process for writing content that ranks and still reads well. You'll learn how to understand what searchers want, build a simple brief, write in a human voice, add original value and edit properly. There's a checklist at the end.

## Start with the person, not the keyword

Before you write a word, ask: who is searching for this, and what do they want to do next?

A person who types "how to write SEO content" probably wants steps. A person who types "SEO content writer Lagos" wants to hire someone. A person who types "what is a content brief" wants a definition and an example. These are different needs, so they need different pages.

That need is called **search intent**. You can discover it with a simple test: search for your keyword and look at what Google already shows.

![Screenshot: a search results page with a People also ask box showing related questions](/images/screenshots/serp-people-also-ask.png)

*Figure 1: Results and "People also ask" questions show what searchers want to know.*

Look for:

- **The page types.** Guides, product pages, lists, videos? Match the type.
- **The questions** in "People also ask". Plan to answer them.
- **The structure** of the top results: headings, length, examples.
- **What's missing.** Where could you be clearer, more current or more practical?

## Step 1: Build a simple brief

A brief is a one-page plan. It stops you wandering, and it makes the writing faster. If you're working with a writer, it's essential.

![Screenshot: a content brief with title, keyword, search intent, audience, questions, outline and links](/images/screenshots/content-brief-doc.png)

*Figure 3: A brief takes ten minutes and saves hours of rewriting.*

Include:

1. **Working title.**
2. **Primary keyword** and a few related phrases.
3. **Search intent.** What does the reader want?
4. **Audience.** Who is it for, and what do they already know?
5. **Questions to answer.** From "People also ask" and customer conversations.
6. **Outline.** The main headings in order.
7. **Original value.** The example, data, photo or advice only you can add.
8. **Internal links.** Which of your pages should it link to?
9. **Call to action.** What should the reader do next?
10. **Length range.** Long enough to cover the topic, not padded.

## Step 2: Open with the answer

The first few lines decide whether people stay. Don't warm up. Say what the page covers and give the main answer straight away, then expand.

**Weak opening:** "In today's digital landscape, many businesses are wondering about the importance of content."

**Strong opening:** "Good SEO content answers one question clearly. Here's how to find that question, plan the page and write it in plain language."

That pattern also helps with search features and AI answers, because it gives a clear, quotable answer. See [how to rank in AI search](/blog/how-to-rank-in-ai-search-geo-aeo/).

## Step 3: Write in plain, human language

Readers on phones, often in a hurry, want clear writing. Here's how to get there.

- **Use short sentences.** If a sentence has three commas, split it.
- **Choose everyday words.** "Use" beats "utilise". "Help" beats "facilitate".
- **Write to "you".** Speak to the reader directly.
- **Use contractions.** "You'll" and "don't" sound human.
- **Keep paragraphs short.** Two to four lines is plenty.
- **Say one thing at a time.**
- **Be specific.** "Our plans start from $500 per month" beats "affordable pricing".
- **Cut filler.** Delete "in order to", "it is important to note that" and "very".

The US government's [plain language guidelines](https://www.plainlanguage.gov/) are a free, practical guide. Their advice works for any audience.

## Step 4: Use headings that help people scan

Most visitors skim before they read. Make it easy.

- **Use clear headings** written as the questions people ask. "How much does SEO cost?" beats "Pricing considerations".
- **Put the main keyword in the title and one heading**, naturally. Don't repeat it in every heading.
- **Use lists** for steps and options, and **tables** for comparisons.
- **Bold a few key phrases**, not whole paragraphs.
- **Add a short summary** at the top of long pages.

Good structure also helps search engines understand your page, which supports your title and description work. See our guide on [title tags and meta descriptions](/blog/title-tags-and-meta-descriptions-that-get-clicks/).

## Step 5: Add something only you can add

This is the step that separates content that ranks from content that sits. If your page says what ten others say, there's no reason to choose it.

Add at least one of these:

- **Your experience.** What you've actually seen, done or tested.
- **Real examples.** A case, a client story (with permission), a before and after.
- **Original data.** Prices, results, customer questions, survey answers.
- **Your own photos or screenshots.**
- **A tool or template.** A checklist, calculator or script.
- **A clear opinion.** A point of view, backed by reasons.

Google's guidance on [helpful, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) stresses first-hand experience and original value. ## Step 6: Use keywords naturally

You don't need a magic keyword count. Use these habits:

- Put the main keyword in the **title**, the **first paragraph** and at least one **heading**, where it reads naturally.
- Use **related phrases and questions** throughout.
- Write the way people talk. If a sentence sounds strange aloud, rewrite it.
- Never stuff keywords. It reads badly and can look spammy.

## Step 7: Link to related pages

Internal links help readers find the next step, and they help search engines understand your site. Add links to your most relevant guides and to the service page that fits. Use descriptive link text. Our guide to [internal linking](/blog/internal-linking-guide/) explains how.

Also link out to trustworthy sources when you rely on a fact. It shows you've done your research.

## Step 8: How long should it be?

There's no perfect word count. Long enough to answer the question fully, and no longer. A simple definition may need 600 words. A full guide may need 2,000. Check what the top results cover, and aim to be more complete or clearer, not just longer.

## Step 9: Edit like a reader

Never publish a first draft. Editing is where good content is made.

1. **Leave it overnight**, if you can. Fresh eyes catch more.
2. **Read it aloud.** You'll hear awkward lines.
3. **Cut the first paragraph** if it only warms up.
4. **Check every fact, number and name.**
5. **Replace vague words** with specifics.
6. **Shorten long sentences and paragraphs.**
7. **Fix the headings** so they tell the story alone.
8. **Check spelling and formatting**, including on mobile.

If you use AI for drafts, treat them as raw material that needs a thorough human edit, fact-check and real examples. Our guide to [using AI for SEO without hurting your rankings](/blog/how-to-use-ai-for-seo-content/) explains a safe workflow.

## Step 10: Publish well

Before you press publish:

- Write a clear **title** and **meta description**.
- Add a descriptive **URL**.
- Add **images** with helpful alt text.
- Include a clear **call to action**.
- Check the page on **mobile**.

After publishing, request indexing in [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console), and check back in a few weeks to see which searches bring impressions. Update the page as you learn.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Writing for search engines, not people.**
- **Copying competitors with small changes.**
- **Padding to hit a word count.**
- **Burying the answer.**
- **Using jargon without explanation.**
- **Publishing without editing or fact-checking.**
- **Ignoring mobile readers.**
- **Never updating old content.**
- **Chasing every keyword with thin pages.** One strong page beats five weak ones.

## A quick checklist

- Do I know who this is for and what they want?
- Is there a brief with questions and an outline?
- Does the page answer the main question in the first few lines?
- Is the language plain, short and human?
- Are the headings clear and scannable?
- Have I added something original: an example, data, photo or opinion?
- Are the facts checked and sources linked?
- Have I linked to related pages?
- Have I edited and read it aloud?
- Is the title, description and mobile view ready?

## Frequently asked questions

### How many keywords should I use?
Focus on one main topic per page, with a primary keyword and natural related phrases. Don't count repetitions.

### Does longer content rank better?
Not automatically. Content that fully answers the question tends to do well. Padding doesn't help.

### Can AI write my SEO content?
AI can help with ideas, outlines and edits, but publishing raw AI text is risky. Add real experience and check everything.

### How often should I publish?
Consistency matters more than volume. One strong page a month beats ten thin ones. Choose a pace you can keep.

### How do I know if the content is working?
Check impressions, clicks and positions in Search Console, plus enquiries or sales from the page. Give new content several weeks.
